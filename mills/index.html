Mills

Four companies, one supplier, and one price for most of what they sell. The Kingdom buys the wheat, sets what the mills pay for it and sets what the flour leaves at, so the margin on the core product is a policy rather than a performance. The operating results duly cluster: much the same plant, much the same ton, much the same revenue for milling it. What separates the four between FY2018 and FY2025 is not milling. It is the price each buyer paid when the state sold the company, and how much of that price is still in invested capital. Strip it out and the order of the four inverts.

How the industry works

A flour mill is not a complicated machine, and almost none of the economics of a Saudi one is decided inside it. The wheat arrives at a price the state sets. Most of the flour leaves at a price the state sets. The quantity in between is an allocation rather than a sale won. Before any ratio here means anything it is worth being exact about which decisions these four companies actually make.

What it takes to sell a bag of flour

A milling company is four things built in order, and each one depends on the one before it. Three of them can be bought. The first is granted.

The margin is set, not won

The General Food Security Authority imports the wheat, holds it, and passes it to the four licensed millers at a single price set for all of them. Under the pricing policy the authority issued in June 2020, that price is SAR 180 a ton, and the authority undertook to hold a difference of SAR 320 a ton between it and the price the millers charge for regulated flour. The gap between what a miller pays and what it receives is therefore not negotiated with anyone. It is granted, to the riyal.

Regulated flour is a defined thing rather than a loose category. It is flour sold in bulk or in sacks of 45 kilograms and up, to buyers the authority has approved, which are mostly bakeries. Packets of ten kilograms and under, the ones a shopper picks up, sit outside the scheme entirely and are priced by whoever makes them.

That single fact explains most of what these accounts look like. You cannot take share by cutting the price of regulated flour, because you do not set it. You cannot be caught out on the wheat, because you did not buy it on a market. Two of the three things that normally decide a manufacturer's fortunes have been removed.

The tell is in the revenue line. Divide each company's revenue by the tons of wheat it milled, and the three that disclose tonnage land within a few percent of one another, year after year. In an industry with real pricing power that figure would fan out. Here it does not, because there is no price to have power over.

Which leaves the question this piece is really about. If the operating economics are handed to all four in the same envelope, why do their returns on capital differ by nearly ten points?

How they make money

With the price of the main product fixed, a miller has three levers and no others: how many tons it can push through the plant it already owns, how much it can earn on the part of the grain that policy does not price, and how little capital it can tie up doing both.

Selling the same ton twice

A ton of wheat does not become a ton of flour. Milling separates it, and only the flour fraction is what the pricing policy is written about. The rest leaves the plant as bran and animal feed, and it leaves at a market price.

So every miller sells the same ton twice, once into a regulated market and once into an open one, and the two halves behave nothing alike. The regulated half is a volume business with a granted margin and no pricing risk. The unregulated half is a genuine commodity business, exposed to the feed market, and it is the part of the core operation where a management team can actually be better or worse than its peers.

Why utilization is the whole growth story

If the price is fixed and the allocation is granted, growth has to come from tons, and tons come from two places: more capacity, or more use of the capacity already standing.

All four now run their mills close to flat out. That is a comfortable position and an awkward one. Comfortable, because a plant near capacity spreads its fixed costs as thinly as they will go; awkward, because the free growth is finished. From here, more tons means more plant, and more plant means more invested capital measured against a margin that policy still sets.

Which makes capital expenditure the most revealing line in this sector's accounts. A miller adding capacity is not chasing a better price. It is buying the right to sell more units at the same one, and that only creates value while the granted margin holds.

A cash business, until recently

The third lever is the working capital, and the striking thing about it is how little of it there is. A milling company is paid almost immediately. Receivables across the four have run at a few days of sales for most of the period covered here, and in several years at less than one. A telecom operator in the same market waits about four months.

That is what selling to an approved list at an administered price does. The buyer has been cleared by the authority, the price is not in dispute, and the product is a staple moving on a fixed schedule. There is no negotiation to slow the payment down and very little credit risk to price, which is how a sector this capital-heavy runs on almost no working capital at all. What the cash cycle does contain is wheat: inventory, not receivables, is the whole of it.

What makes the line worth watching is that it has started to move. Receivables at all four have risen in each of the last two years, from a figure that rounded to nothing into one that shows up on the balance sheet, and they have moved together. A change that arrives across an entire industry at once is rarely a collections problem at any one company. It is more likely a change in who the customer is, and there is a dated candidate for it. The authority opened flour exports to the licensed millers in November 2024, and the small-pack retail market sits outside the price scheme altogether. Selling to a distributor, a retailer or an overseas buyer means selling on terms. Selling to an approved bakery did not.

Where the cash goes

Every riyal earned arrives at the same fork: back into the plant, or out to shareholders. In this sector the fork has a third arm the others do not: repaying the debt the buyer used to acquire the company in the first place.

The price the buyer paid is still on the balance sheet

The state did not float these companies. It sold them, in two rounds, to private consortia, and each buyer paid what it thought the license and the plant were worth. Where the price exceeded the book value of what was bought, the difference was recorded as goodwill and acquired intangibles, and it has sat on the balance sheet ever since.

That is not an accounting detail. Return on invested capital divides profit by the capital employed to earn it, and a purchase premium enters the denominator without adding a single ton of milling capacity. Two of the four carry a large one. Two carry almost none. Their plants are near enough the same size.

So the reported return on capital of a Saudi miller answers two questions at once. How good is this business, and how much did its owner pay for it? Only the first is about milling. Separate them and the order changes completely: measured on operating capital alone, the company with the lowest reported return is the best operator in the cohort.

None of which makes the purchase price irrelevant. It was real money, it was paid, and today's shareholders inherited it. But anyone comparing these four is choosing between businesses whose operations are nearly interchangeable and whose balance sheets are not, and it is worth knowing which of the two a ratio is describing.

Modern Mills 2284

No acquisition goodwill and the smallest capital base in the sector, which is why it posts the highest reported return on capital and the widest spread over the cost of it. It got there by paying its equity out before listing, and it carries the heaviest debt load here as a result.

The one paying it down

Arabian Mills 2285

The widest gross margin in the cohort, earned on the second-largest milling capacity, against a purchase premium and a debt package to work off. It has cut borrowings hard and retained more of its profit than anyone else here, which is the same decision seen twice.

The clean one

Fourth Mills 2286

The smallest of the four, and the only one with no bank borrowings at all and next to no purchase premium. It runs its plant harder than anyone else in the cohort and turns more of each riyal of sales into profit, on a fraction of the headcount.

What decides the next two years

Whether the wheat support survives its next review

The commitment to hold the price differential ran to 12 July 2025 and then lapsed, and the support has carried on unchanged since. Every spread over cost of capital in this sector sits on top of it. The millers are not wholly unprotected: the Ministry of Finance agreed to compensate them if the law, the implementing regulations, the pricing policy or the license period were changed, which is a real backstop against a rewrite. The gap in it is the case that matters most. On the terms disclosed at listing, a complete lifting of the subsidy after July 2025 is not a change the companies can claim against. That is the one version of this risk the state has not agreed to absorb.

Whether the purchase premiums are ever earned

Two of the four carry a large privatization premium in invested capital, and it does not shrink as debt is repaid. On the plants themselves all four earn comfortably more than their cost of capital; on the price their owners paid, two of them are still working. Watch for either an impairment, which resets the denominator in a single line, or enough profit growth to absorb it, which takes years.

Whether growth has anywhere left to come from

All four now run close to full capacity and the free growth is finished. The next ton has to be bought with capital, at a margin the state still sets. The first meaningful capacity additions since privatization have already begun, and the sector's returns over the next few years will largely be the arithmetic of what those additions cost against a margin that does not move.

Whether the unregulated half of the ton outgrows the regulated half

Bran, feed and durum sell at market prices, and the authority only opened flour exports to the licensed millers in November 2024, on condition that they export surplus capacity and refund the wheat subsidy on whatever leaves the country. That is the only part of this business where a management team can be better than its peers rather than the same as them. It is also the only part exposed to a commodity market, which cuts both ways.

This is the rare sector where the cohort is the industry: all four milling companies licensed to process the Kingdom's subsidized wheat are listed, all four report to 31 December, and all four are covered here. A “sector median” on this site is therefore the median of the whole industry rather than of a sample. The histories are not equally long: the companies were carved out of the state grains organization and sold in 2020 and 2021, and the earliest years on each sheet are pre-privatization carve-out accounts, which is why several series begin part way across the period.
