Car Rental

A car rental company owns nothing that is scarce: ordinary vehicles bought at a price the manufacturer sets, hired out in a market anyone with capital can enter, sold at a price the used-car market sets. Between FY2018 and FY2025 the three listed operators more than doubled the vehicles on the road and paid for them with borrowing, and the margin between what those fleets earn and what the capital costs has closed by more than four fifths at every one of them since FY2021. All three still clear that hurdle, by less in FY2025 than in any other year covered here.

How the industry works

There is no license here, no allocation, no spectrum and no plant that takes four years to build. A rental company buys the same vehicles anyone else can buy and hires them out in a market with no barrier worth the name. Before any ratio means anything it is worth being exact about where the money in a business like that comes from, because it is not where the revenue line suggests.

Four steps, and the company sets one price

A rental company is four things done in order, and the striking thing about the list is how few of them involve a decision a competitor could not copy the same afternoon.

The vehicle is the cost

Start with the line that decides everything, which is not the revenue and not the margin. It is depreciation.

A vehicle is bought, held for three or four years while it earns, and then sold. Over that period it loses most of what was paid for it, and the loss is charged against profit a month at a time. Across the three listed operators, more than half of last year's EBITDA went straight back out again as depreciation and amortization. Not most of the growth in it. More than half of the whole of it.

That has a consequence for how the sector should be read. EBITDA is a useful measure where the asset behind it was bought once and lasts for decades: a mill, a fibre network, a tower. It is close to meaningless here, because the asset it is struck before is consumed on a four-year cycle and then has to be bought again. A rental company reporting a wide EBITDA margin has told you almost nothing until it has told you what its vehicles cost it.

Which is why the useful margin in this sector sits below the depreciation line rather than above it, and why the two prices at either end of the drawing matter more than anything the company does in between. Buy well or sell well and the whole business works. Neither price is set by the company.

How they make money

One vehicle, three ways to be paid for it, and the third is the one nobody counts. Separating them is most of the difference between a reading of these accounts and a reading of the ratios computed from them.

Selling the same car three times

Leasing and rental are usually described as two ends of one business. They are closer to two businesses sharing a car park.

A long lease is a financing arrangement with a vehicle attached. The customer is a company or a government body, the term runs in years, the rate is fixed at the start, and utilization is not really a question: the vehicle is out whether anyone is driving it or not. What the lessor is exposed to is the customer's credit and its own funding cost.

A daily rental is a hospitality business with a vehicle attached. The customer is a person, the term is a weekend, the rate moves with demand, and every day the vehicle sits on the forecourt is a day it earns nothing at all. What the renter is exposed to is footfall, seasonality, and the branch network it has to keep staffed in order to catch them.

The two behave differently in almost every part of the accounts, from the receivable to the shape of a bad year, and the mix a company runs tells you more about how its next downturn will look than its margin does.

A third of the revenue is a used car

Then the third door, which quietly distorts every margin comparison in the sector.

Every vehicle is eventually sold, and when it is, the proceeds go through revenue and the carrying value goes through cost of sales. That is ordinary accounting and there is nothing wrong with it. But it means a large share of what these companies report as revenue is a used car changing hands at close to what the accounts already said it was worth, at a margin of a few percent.

Across the three that trade is close to a third of the top line, and it has been growing. The cost of vehicles sold has more than doubled as a share of revenue since FY2019, and the sector's reported gross margin has fallen by roughly fourteen points over the same period.

Read straight, that looks like a price war. It is not. Take the disposals out of both the top and the bottom of the ratio and the gross margin on the business of hiring vehicles out is far wider and has moved far less. What has changed is the mix, and specifically how many vehicles are being cycled off the fleet each year, which is a consequence of how fast the fleet was built rather than of what anyone is charging. Two of the three, on that adjusted basis, turn out to be running the same gross margin to within a fifth of a point.

It also means the reported gross margins are not comparable across the three without that adjustment. They sell very different proportions of their fleets each year, and they book very different margins when they do.

Where the three stop being different companies

Now put the three side by side on one measure at a time, working down the income statement. Something happens on the way down that is worth stopping for.

On gross margin they look like three different businesses, several points apart. On EBITDA margin the distance is smaller. By EBIT margin, which is the first line that charges each of them properly for the vehicle wearing out, the three sit within half a point of one another. On return on invested capital they are within about a point, and on what is left of that return after the cost of the capital, all three are pinned against zero.

This is the finding the piece is built on. The differences between these companies are real, and almost all of them live above the depreciation line. The depreciation line is where the economics of renting a vehicle are actually settled. Charge each company for its cars at the rate its own accounts say they are losing value, and there is very little left to choose between them.

That is what you would expect of an industry with no barrier to entry, a commodity asset and three competent operators chasing the same corporate fleets. It has taken a few years to arrive: in FY2019 the three were more than twelve points apart on EBIT margin. It is here now.

Where the cash goes

Every riyal arrives at the same fork as everywhere else on this site, with one difference that changes the arithmetic underneath it: one arm is not optional. A network can be run for another year without being spent on. A fleet cannot.

The capital spending you cannot see

Look up capital expenditure for any of these three and the number is close to nothing: around one percent of revenue, in a sector that has more than doubled the vehicles it owns in seven years. Both of those are true, and reconciling them is the single most useful thing to understand about these accounts.

Rental vehicles are trading stock as much as they are fixed assets. They are bought and sold continuously as part of running the business, so the cash that buys them runs through operating activities rather than through the investing section. The acquisition-of-property line in the cash flow statement is picking up branches, workshops and computers. It is not picking up the cars.

So the ordinary construction of free cash flow, operating cash flow less that capital expenditure line, is not measuring what it usually measures. It is measuring how fast the fleet is growing. A company buying more vehicles than it sells reports negative free cash flow while it is doing rather well, and a company that stops growing reports a large positive figure the following year without having become a better business. Taken together the three have reported negative free cash flow in each of the last four years, and every one of them was profitable in every one of them.

None of this is hidden. It is all in the statements. But it does mean that the most quoted cash metric in equity research is, in this sector, measuring the wrong thing, and that the honest way to see what the fleet absorbs is to build it back up from the movement in the vehicles themselves.

Who paid for the fleet

The fleet did not come out of retained earnings. It came out of borrowing, and the timing was unkind.

In the first years covered here the three carried almost no debt between them. Interest-bearing debt across the sector is now measured in billions and roughly matches shareholders' funds. Interest cover has gone from a figure high enough to be meaningless to single digits at all three.

The cost of that capital moved the other way at the same time. The risk-free rate rose, the betas rose as the shares began trading with real volume, and the sector's weighted average cost of capital has more than doubled from its trough. The denominator of the question this site asks was rising while the numerator was coming down.

That is the whole of the spread compression, and it is worth being clear that it is two separate things rather than one. Returns on capital fell because the fleet grew faster than the earnings on it. The cost of capital rose because money got dearer and these became more volatile shares. Only the first is something a management team can do much about, and it is the one that gets the least attention.

Paid later than it used to be

The last thing worth watching is the least dramatic, which is usually where these things start.

A rental company used to be paid quickly. Two of the three now wait around four months for their money, having waited a little over two in FY2019, and both have lengthened in each of the last three years. That is a large change in a business whose asset is already financed, because every extra day of receivable is another day of a vehicle that has been paid for by the company and not yet paid for by anybody else.

The likeliest explanation is in the revenue mix rather than in collections. Leasing to companies and to government bodies means invoicing on terms; a weekend rental settles on a card at the counter. The check on that reading is the third company, which collects fastest in the sector and has shortened rather than lengthened across the period. Whatever is happening here is not happening to everyone at once, which is what separates it from the milling sector, where the same measure turned at all four companies inside the same two years.

For scale, the millers covered elsewhere on this site are paid inside a fortnight, and the telecom operators wait four months or more. In six years two of these three have travelled most of the distance from the first shape to the second. Nothing in the accounts says that is a crisis, and it is not. It is simply the line that would move first if the customers on the other end of those contracts began paying more slowly still, on balance sheets now carrying real debt.

The three operators

No license, no allocation, no scarce input, and three companies buying vehicles from the same manufacturers. Below is what each one actually runs, on the same four questions the sequence asked: what it owns, what it hires out, who paid for it, and how it leaves.

What decides the next two years

Whether the spread survives another year of this

The margin between what these fleets earn and what the capital costs has closed by more than four fifths at all three since FY2021, and FY2025 is the narrowest year of the eight for every one of them. It does not have far left to go. Two things would widen it again: rates coming down, which is not in anyone's gift here, or the fleet pausing long enough for earnings to catch up with the capital already committed. Watch which of the three blinks first, and note that the one that keeps buying while the others stop will print the worst numbers for a year or two and may be making the better decision.

What the used cars fetch

Close to a third of this industry's revenue is a vehicle being sold, and the price it fetches is the retrospective verdict on several years of depreciation assumptions. The Kingdom's used-car market has absorbed a fleet that has more than doubled, and every one of those vehicles comes back to it eventually. If residual values soften it arrives in these accounts twice: once as a thinner margin on the disposals, and again as a depreciation charge that turns out to have been too light on everything still on the road.

Whether the receivable keeps lengthening

Two of the three have added the better part of two months to their collection periods since FY2019, and the likeliest reason is a revenue mix tilting towards leasing to companies and to government bodies. That is a change in who the customer is rather than a collections failure, and at this level it is not alarming. It would become so if it carried on, because the vehicle behind every unpaid invoice has already been paid for once, on balance sheets that are now carrying real debt.

Whether three is the right number of companies

There is no license here and no scarce input. Nothing stops a fourth operator, a manufacturer's own captive fleet or a ride-hailing platform buying vehicles at the same discount and competing for the same corporate contracts. You do not need a fourth entrant to see what that does, because the pressure is already in the exhibits above: three companies with different networks, different revenue mixes and very different balance sheets have arrived at very nearly the same return on capital, which is what a market with no barrier to entry eventually does to everyone standing in it.
